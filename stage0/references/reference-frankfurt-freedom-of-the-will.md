## 1. Mental Model (read first)

Frankfurt's claim is not "some desires are stronger than others" but **wanting is not flat** — desires
come in orders, and only a creature capable of forming a *second-order volition* (not just a
second-order desire) is a person rather than a "wanton." The distinction is not one of intensity: a
desire reported with total confidence, in the moment, is still first-order if it has not been
appointed to govern action against a rival desire. The order of a desire is a fact about its
structure, not about how strongly or sincerely it was stated.

## 2. Frameworks & Structure

### 2.1 First-order desire — wanting to do or not do something
A first-order desire is simply a desire to do or not do one thing or another; most animals have the
capacity for these. A statement "A wants to X" taken alone says nothing about whether this desire is
paramount among A's other desires or will actually move A to act — A may want to X while strongly
preferring something else, and may want to X despite the fact that, when he acts, some other desire
is what actually moves him. **Reporting a first-order desire is not the same event as it governing
action.**

### 2.2 Second-order desire — wanting to want
A second-order desire is wanting to have (or not have) a certain first-order desire, or wanting a
certain desire not to be one's own. No animal other than man appears to have the capacity for this
reflective self-evaluation. Critically, a second-order desire does not entail already holding the
first-order desire in question in every case, and — more importantly for grading purposes — merely
wanting to *have* a desire is weaker than wanting that desire to be *effective*: a physician may want
to feel an addict's desire for a drug without wanting that desire to move him to take it. A
second-order desire, on its own, never settles which first-order desire is to act; it can sit open
indefinitely without resolving.

### 2.3 Second-order volition — appointing a desire to govern action
A second-order volition is the stronger case: A wants a certain desire to *be his will* — to be the
desire that actually moves him to act, not merely one he has or feels. This is a decisive act of
identification, not a report: "he wants this desire to be effective — that is, to provide the motive
in what he actually does." A second-order volition is what allows an unwilling addict, who has
conflicting first-order desires (wants the drug, wants to refrain), to coherently say the desire that
drives him to take the drug is a force "other than his own" — because he has identified with, and
so made his will, the opposing first-order desire instead.

### 2.4 The wanton — first-order (and possibly second-order) desire without volition
A wanton has first-order desires, and may even have second-order desires, but has formed no
second-order volition: he "does not care about his will." His desires move him without its being
true that he wants to be moved by them or would prefer to be moved by others. A wanton can be fully
rational and can deliberate — the defect is not cognitive, it is that he never asks which of his
inclinations should be the one that wins. All nonhuman animals with desires, and very young children,
are wantons; adult humans can act wantonly to varying degrees without being wantons across the board.
**A wanton reporting a desire — however confidently, however often — has not thereby formed a
second-order volition; wantonness and confidence are independent axes.**

### 2.5 The unwilling addict vs. the wanton addict — same first-order conflict, different structure
Two addicts, same physiological compulsion, same conflicting first-order desires (take the drug /
refrain). The unwilling addict has a second-order volition: he wants his desire to refrain to be his
effective will, and is "violated" when the desire to take it wins instead — a helpless bystander to a
force he does not identify with. The wanton addict has no such volition: his actions "reflect the
economy of his first-order desires" with no concern for which one should win; he is not even neutral
between them, since neutrality presupposes an evaluative standpoint he has not taken. **Identical
first-order data, different second-order structure, different verdict about which desire may be
treated as this person's will.**

### 2.6 Freedom of the will — conformity of will to second-order volition
Freedom of *action* is being free to do what one wants to do. Freedom of the *will*, by analogy, is
being free to want what one wants to want — to have the will (the effective first-order desire) that
one's second-order volition calls for. A person enjoys this freedom when their will conforms to their
second-order volitions; its lack is felt as estrangement from oneself, or as being a passive bystander
to the forces that move you, even while remaining free to act.

### 2.7 No theoretical limit to the order series, but a real terminus in practice
There is no upper bound on how many orders of desire a reflective creature could in principle form
(wanting to want to want to X, and so on) — nothing but common sense and fatigue stops the regress,
and unchecked pursuit of ever-higher orders is itself a way to destroy oneself as a person. But a
decisive second-order volition terminates the series without needing to address every higher order
explicitly: once someone has, without reservation, identified with a first-order desire as their
will, questions about the third order and beyond become moot — "resounding" back down through the
hierarchy rather than requiring an infinite explicit regress. **A single explicit, decisive
appointment is sufficient; the check is not "how many orders were considered" but "was one ever
decisively appointed."**

### 2.8 Second-order volitions need not be moral, careful, or well-grounded
Forming a second-order volition is not necessarily a moral act and may be capricious or
under-considered — "there is no essential restriction on the kind of basis, if any, upon which they
are formed" (Frankfurt's own footnote, `§II` n.6). **Grading whether something has reached
second-order volition is a structural check on the kind of statement made, not a quality or
sincerity check on how well-reasoned it was.**

## 3. Decision Rules & Judgment

- Before writing a stated preference into anything meant to be treated as settled, classify it:
  does the statement report a current inclination (`§2.1`), an evaluation of a desire without
  appointing one to act (`§2.2`), or an explicit, forward-standing appointment of which desire
  governs this class of decision even against a louder rival at the moment of choice (`§2.3`)?
  Only the third grade may be treated as settled.
- Confidence, repetition, and vividness of a stated desire are evidence about `§2.1`, never evidence
  of `§2.3`. A first-order desire stated with total conviction is still first-order (`§2.4`); do not
  let intensity of statement substitute for the distinct act of appointment.
- If the same person's statements conflict across occasions (wants X now, said last week they want
  to not-want-X), that is ordinary evidence of unresolved second-order deliberation (`§2.2`), not a
  contradiction to be resolved by picking whichever was stated most recently or most forcefully.
- Do not manufacture a second-order volition on the person's behalf by inferring it from a
  first-order answer, no matter how natural the inference feels — identifying which desire is to
  govern is the one act only the person themselves is positioned to perform (`§2.3`, `§2.6`).
- A construct that has not cleared second-order volition is not thereby worthless — record it as
  what it is (a first- or second-order desire) and leave it open rather than discarding it or
  silently promoting it.

## 4. Key Takeaways

1. Desires have structure, not just strength: first-order (want to do X), second-order (want to want
   X), second-order volition (appoint a desire to be one's effective will) are three different kinds
   of statement, not three intensities of the same kind.
2. A wanton — human or otherwise — can hold first-order and even second-order desires with total
   sincerity while never forming a second-order volition; the defect is structural, not a lack of
   confidence or reflection.
3. The unwilling-addict / wanton-addict pair shows that identical first-order conflict data supports
   opposite verdicts about whose will is doing what, depending purely on second-order structure.
4. Freedom of the will is conformity between one's effective desire and one's second-order volition —
   a different question from freedom of action, and not settled by knowing what someone did.
5. A decisive second-order volition terminates the theoretical regress of higher orders; the
   practical check is whether one decisive appointment was ever made, not how many orders were
   examined.
6. Second-order volitions carry no built-in guarantee of being moral, careful, or well-reasoned —
   grading their presence is a structural classification, not a quality judgment.
7. This taxonomy grades claims about a person's objective/preference, and only that; it supplies no
   stock, no arrow, no model class, and does not enter Stage 0's fixed Meadows → Pearl → Page
   pipeline.
